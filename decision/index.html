01 · What Jev is

A model that picks from your list.

Jev gives back one answer from a set you define, with a confidence score attached. It never streams words.

TypeSafe calls Jev a System One model, after the fast, automatic kind of human thinking. You give it a situation and a fixed set of answers. It returns a typed result: a choice from your list, a yes or no, or a score, each with a calibrated confidence. An LLM is the slow, deliberate kind. You want one when the answer is open ended, and you overpay for one when the answer is one of five labels.

03 · The prompt

Find the decisions in your own work.

Paste this into Claude or ChatGPT. It asks you a few questions, then ranks where a decision model would save you the most.

It works best in a chat that already knows your work, like a Claude project or ChatGPT with memory on. If it doesn't know enough, it interviews you first.

I want to find the places in my work where a decision model would do a better job than a large language model (LLM).

Some context first. A decision model, like Jev from TypeSafe, does not write text. You give it a situation and a fixed set of possible answers, and it returns one of three things, each with a calibrated confidence score:
- a choice from a list (which category, which team, which template)
- a yes or no (is this spam, is this lead qualified, does this need a human)
- a score (how urgent, how likely to convert, how good a match)
It is much cheaper and faster than an LLM, and the answer is always one of the options I gave it. It is a poor fit for open-ended writing, multi-step reasoning, or anything where the possible answers aren't known in advance.

Your job is to audit my work and find the repeated judgement calls that fit that shape.

STEP 1. Learn my work.
If you already know my job, business, tools and workflows from our past conversations or memory, summarise what you know in 5 bullet points and ask me to correct anything that's wrong. If you don't know enough, interview me. Ask one question at a time, 8 questions at most. Cover: what I do, what I repeat every week, what lands in my inbox, forms, CRM or queues, where I or my team sort, tag, approve, reject, prioritise or route things, and where I already use AI to make a call rather than to write something.

STEP 2. List every repeated judgement step.
A judgement step is any moment where a person or an AI looks at something and picks from a known set of outcomes. Put them all in one table with these columns:
1. Step: a short name
2. Situation input: exactly what the model would look at (an email, a ticket, a lead record, a document, a transcript)
3. Answer set: the full fixed list of outcomes. For a score, give the scale and what each end means. Include an "other / not sure" option where one is needed.
4. Confidence threshold: how confident the answer must be before it's acted on automatically, and why that level
5. Below the threshold: what happens then. Either it goes to an LLM with more context, or to a named person
6. Volume: roughly how many times per week
7. Done by today: me, my team, an LLM call, or nobody because it's too slow or too expensive
8. Estimated saving: hours per week, plus money per month if it's currently an LLM call or paid time. Show your working and mark every number as an estimate.

STEP 3. Rank the top 3 to try first.
Rank by value and ease: high volume, a clean answer set, a low cost when it's wrong, and a clear fallback. For each of the three, give me:
- a one-paragraph plan I could test this week
- 10 real example inputs I should collect to check its accuracy
- how I'll know it worked: the metric and the target

STEP 4. Flag the bad fits.
List the steps you looked at and rejected, and say why. Reject anything that needs open-ended writing, multi-step reasoning or planning, knowledge that isn't in the input, or an answer set that keeps changing. Say so plainly when an LLM or a person is still the right tool.

Rules:
- Don't invent facts about my work. If you're guessing, say so and ask.
- Keep every answer set small, and make sure it covers every case.
- Keep it practical. I want things I can test this week, not a strategy deck.
