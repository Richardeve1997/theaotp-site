02Move 1

Build one narrow feature first.

Cal AI's first version did one thing. You took a photo of your food and it told you the calories.

Zach described version one on My First Million #687 (17 Mar 2025, about 15:09). You took a picture, it told you the calories, and it showed a daily breakdown. "That was it. One feature." He says they added more features once they saw people liked it. In a Q&A with CEO Insider he gives the same blueprint: one feature, take a picture of food and get the calories, and build the whole app around it.

Persona is narrow in a different way. TechCrunch says it can order food or rides, take notes, book travel and answer emails, so the jobs are broad. The narrow part is the way in: it launched as a free beta you use by text message in iMessage, and an app is in the works.

Use it in your business

  • Name the one job. Write what your product does for a customer in one sentence. If the sentence needs "and", you have two features.
  • Cut everything else from version one. Cal AI's first version had the photo, the calories and a daily breakdown. That was all.
  • Add the next feature after people use the first. Zach's order was build it, see that people like it, then add more.
Cal AI is more than one feature today. Its App Store screenshots show progress charts and friend groups too. The move is about version one.
03Move 2

Don't train a model. Build a new way to use one.

Cal AI didn't train its own AI. It built an app around models that already existed.

Zach told Nathan Latka (uploaded 5 Jun 2025, about 1:28) that the app is only possible now because of AI, mainly through APIs like ChatGPT: "We did not train our own model to do this." On My First Million #687 he said people dismiss apps like Cal AI as AI wrappers, and that it's true they started as one. He calls starting that way "something all apps should do" (about 14:27) and compares it to dropshipping: prove the idea on someone else's product, then build your own once it works.

The early build leaned on ChatGPT. TechCrunch reported in March 2025 that the app used models from Anthropic and OpenAI, plus RAG (the model checks reference data) to improve accuracy. Zach told TechCrunch that different models are better with different foods.

Persona's new way in is a $179 wristband: press its button, or flick your wrist, and talk to the assistant. TechCrunch says the model runs in Persona's cloud, not on the device. The band is a pre-order, expected in December.

Use it in your business

  • Rent the model. Call an existing one through its API. Zach says starting this way is something all apps should do.
  • Shrink what you ask the customer for. Cal AI asks for a photo. Persona asks for a text. What is the smallest thing your customer can give you to get the result?
  • Spend your effort on the experience. The screen, the thread or the device is the part customers see. Design that.
04Move 3

Pay real people to use it on camera.

Creators were Cal AI's biggest channel. Zach says they took it to about $2 million a month.

On My First Million #802 (5 Mar 2026, about 34:03), Zach says influencer marketing started Cal AI and took it really far, to about $2 million a month. Then growth plateaued. What got them past it, he says, was performance ads on Instagram, TikTok and Facebook, and ads fuel most of the growth now.

He said the same thing to Nathan Latka in 2025: influencers on TikTok and Instagram were the biggest channel, with about 250 on retainer, each working Cal AI into their content every month (about 13:46). In the CEO Insider Q&A he says creators get full creative freedom. Cal AI only asks them to include the app naturally in their usual content.

How the deals work

In a 2:41 clip posted by TheBrettWay Clips (11 Oct 2026), Zach explains how he prices a creator. The clip's description calls the pricing an illustrative example and says rates and results vary, so treat the prices as a model, not as Cal AI's rates.

  • 1Work out what a thousand views is worth.What you pay per thousand views has to stay below what you earn per thousand views.
  • 2Predict the views.Look at the creator's recent videos for other brands and estimate what yours will get. The clip's example is a creator whose last ten videos average 50,000 views.
  • 3Pay for the video upfront.At $2 per thousand views, 50,000 views comes to about $100. Creators usually push back and ask for more.
  • 4Offer a bundle or a view floor.Zach offers four videos paid upfront. He says that brings the price down a lot and gets the creator posting every month. The other option is a minimum-view clause: you pay what they ask, and they guarantee the views or keep posting.

What the posts look like

The posts I looked at all have the same shape: a creator points a phone at a meal and the app shows the result. Make the result easy to film. Then get the product on screen early. Alfie Dickens, who built the influencer program behind Cal AI, says one of its biggest rules is to get the app on screen inside the first 15 seconds (Superwall, 25 Aug 2026, about 22:21). Here are five public creator posts. calai.app also has a section called "Used by your favorite fitness influencers".

Use it in your business

  • Run the per-view math before you pay anyone. If what you'd pay per thousand views is more than a thousand views earns you, don't make the deal.
  • Give creators something they can film in one take. Cal AI shows its result in seconds. What does your product show?
  • Plan for a ceiling. Creators took Cal AI to about $2 million a month. Then growth plateaued until paid ads picked up.
05Move 4

Test everything. Think in funnels.

Zach says moving the sign-up screen from the start of onboarding to the end was the biggest change.

Nathan Latka asked what he had eliminated from traditional onboarding flows to cut friction. Zach's answer (about 11:29) was moving the sign-in screens to the end, because there was a lot of drop-off there: "That was the biggest by far." In the Reel I said users were bought in by then. Zach's own explanation is close. Some of the questions his team added to onboarding, like which diet you follow, didn't change what the user got, and conversion still went up. His hypothesis is that the user invests more time (about 7:57).

They kept stacking tests. A "thank you for trusting us" screen raised conversion. So did a screen saying you'll lose weight faster with Cal AI. Animating those screens raised it again. He calls the whole thing a game of making hypotheses and testing them (about 8:12 to 8:36). He's candid that the team mostly ran on intuition at first and now tests every new feature.

His tools were Mixpanel for tests and Superwall for paywalls, and the team ran hundreds of paywall tests (about 8:55 and 10:31 to 11:10). Zach's estimate was that 20 to 25 percent of new users went into a trial or paid (about 9:46).

Use it in your business

  • Move the account request to the end. Ask for the sign-up, or the card, after the customer has put in the effort. Test it against your current order.
  • Write the hypothesis, then test it. Zach's team added questions, then two screens, then animation, and tested each one.
  • Try adding a question. At Cal AI, questions that changed nothing in the app still lifted conversion. It may not hold for you, so test it.
This fits a consumer app with a free trial. If you sell to businesses, test the new order before you copy it.

07What to do now

Pick one move. Run it this week.

Reading it is the easy part. Start where you are.

  • If your product tries to do too much: start at 02 and write down the one job.
  • If you're about to train your own model: start at 03 and check whether an existing one will do.
  • If you have a product and no audience: start at 04 and do the per-view math before you pay a creator.
  • If people start your sign-up and don't finish: start at 05 and test moving the account request to the end.
  • If you want it from Zach himself: start with the three interviews at the top of 06.
