01Start here

One interview. Twenty tips.

Felix Rieseberg has led engineering for Claude.ai, Cowork and Claude Code Desktop at Anthropic. In October he spent 49 minutes with Marina Mogilko on Silicon Valley Girl talking about how he uses Claude now.

I watched the whole thing and pulled out the tips you can use this week. Seventeen come from that interview, two from his How I AI episode with Claire Vo, and one from Anthropic's own Cowork walkthrough. Every tip has its clip cued to the second it's said, so you hear it from the source and not just from me.

02Ask for the tools

Ask for the tools, not the title.

Telling Claude it's a world-class expert mostly gets you the vocabulary. Asking how the best people in the field work gets you their method.

1

Drop the "you're an expert" prompt.

Felix never opens with "you're an excellent engineer". He calls it a 2024 habit. Tell a model to act like a very good accountant and it starts to role play, and role play gets you the vocabulary. He wants the techniques, the tools and the knowledge a professional would use.

So he asks Claude which tools someone at the top of the field would use, then tells it to use the same ones.

"I never tell Claude, 'You're an excellent whatever.'"Felix at 25:10
In the Reel
Silicon Valley Girl

2

Copy the pro's steps, not their result.

The naive prompt is "make this good". Felix gets more by asking how the best in the field work and having Claude take the same steps. His example is 3D and motion video: the big jump came when people told Claude to use Blender, the tool professional animators use.

Writing works the same way. His friend Adam Davidson, who was a staff writer at a number of publications, asks Claude for the strongest counterargument to his piece.

"just tell Claude to use Blender."Felix at 10:31

03Make it prove itself

Make Claude prove itself wrong.

Claude can sound sure and still be wrong. Felix's answer is more Claude: critics that attack the work, and a judge that sorts their notes.

3

Run an adversarial review.

When he's written something, Felix tells Claude to fire off five threads, each one trying to prove the piece wrong: the facts, the framing, anything he misunderstood. He compares it to red teaming or short selling.

Then another Claude acts as the judge and decides which criticisms are valid, and only those go back to the Claude that made the work. When the critics only find small things, he knows the work is good.

"please fire off five threads"Felix at 14:14
Silicon Valley Girl

Open on YouTube at 13:01

4

Use a cheaper model for the critics.

Finding flaws is easier than making something, for people and for models. Felix says you can take a step down in intelligence for the reviewers and still get very good results, so a five-critic review doesn't have to run on your most expensive model.

"take a step down in intelligence and still get very good results."Felix at 13:48
Silicon Valley Girl

Open on YouTube at 13:47

5

Never let a session grade its own work.

Felix trusts Claude with a lot. What he doesn't trust is one session judging what it just made. He looks for hard outside evidence that the work did its job, and in a pinch he uses another Claude session as the judge.

"the current session tends to be quite bad at being a being a judge"Felix at 26:46
Silicon Valley Girl

Open on YouTube at 26:10

6

Hand over fact-checking completely.

Fact-checking is the one job he trusts Claude with 100%, with a single agent and no extra rounds. The instruction: find a primary source for every number he mentions, and check that each one is a good primary source.

Run it on your next deck or post before it goes out. The prompt is in section 09.

"I need you to find primary sources for every number I mention."Felix at 17:28
Silicon Valley Girl

Open on YouTube at 17:13

7

Grade the route, not a lucky answer.

Felix's example is a weather report. If the model guesses the temperature and happens to be right, that should still fail. If it finds the right location and a reputable data source and ends up half a degree off, that should score higher.

When you check Claude's work, check how it got there as well as what it says.

Silicon Valley Girl

Open on YouTube at 7:37

04Give it everything

Give it the whole business.

Felix doesn't build a team of agents with a slice of data each. He gives one generalist Claude everything, through tools it can call when it needs them.

8

Skip the cast of characters.

Felix's spicy take is that people overdo agents. Models are strong generalists: a custom model and harness won silver at the Math Olympiad, and a few months later the general models won gold. Your "data scientist" agent has the same intelligence as any other, so making up a cast of roles first can hold you back.

He still splits work into projects, by the piece of work rather than by role.

"I think we're kind of over-rotating on this agents thing."Felix at 17:48
Silicon Valley Girl

Open on YouTube at 17:48

9

Give it the whole picture, through tools.

An engineering manager who only sees the code is far less useful than one who also hears customer calls and sees usage and business data. Claude is the same, so don't hand each agent a slice.

If that sounds like too much data, the answer is tools. Give Claude a tool for each source and write down clearly what's behind it, so it pulls only what it needs. Models have got much better at deciding when to call a tool, so Felix no longer decides up front what data a task gets.

Silicon Valley Girl

Open on YouTube at 19:08

10

Think about the model's experience.

Product people design for user experience. Felix also thinks about model experience: pretend for a second that you're Claude and you've been handed the task. You'd soon find you want the raw data, a computer, and maybe the ability to install some software. Then set it up that way.

"I like to consider the model experience"Felix at 22:21
Silicon Valley Girl

Open on YouTube at 22:19

11

Stop being the conveyor belt.

A year and a half ago Felix was finding crashes one at a time and handing each one to Claude to fix. Then it dawned on him that he was just moving work from one place to another.

The fix was to give Claude all the crash data and let it work out what needs fixing and what doesn't. That takes more work to set up, and he says it's worth it.

"I'm just a conveyor belt for Claude"Felix at 23:00
Silicon Valley Girl

Open on YouTube at 22:47

12

Start with the desktop app and the browser.

His step one for anyone starting today: download the Claude desktop app and let Claude use your computer, with its built-in browser. Forget connectors and data exports at first.

Once a workflow proves itself, ask whether Claude could get something better than clicking around a dashboard, like a connector.

"step one, download the desktop app."Felix at 24:09
Silicon Valley Girl

Open on YouTube at 24:07

05Teach it your taste

Turn your feedback into rules.

You give feedback all day. Felix has Claude collect it, boil it down and find the rules you didn't know you had. This is the tip from the Reel.

13

Turn your feedback into principles.

Round one: have Claude assemble all the feedback you've ever given, from Slack, GitHub or wherever it lives. Round two: condense it into a list of rules or principles you care about. Round three is the one people miss: ask for the principles you might believe but don't know you believe.

Then use that list as an input to every future review. Felix says it has helped specialise the feedback individual sessions give.

"what are the principles that maybe you might believe but you don't actually know you believe."Felix at 28:59
In the Reel
Silicon Valley Girl

Open on YouTube at 28:45

14

Run Claude side by side before you hand over.

If you don't trust it yet, don't hand over the reins overnight. For a few rounds, write your own feedback first, then check what Claude would have said. Where the two differ shows you what to sharpen.

He points out that even one person gives different feedback on a Monday than on a Friday, so don't expect a perfect match.

15

Ask for the plan before it touches your files.

This one is from Anthropic's own getting-started video. Point Cowork at a folder, describe the job, and ask it to show you the plan before it changes anything. You fix what's wrong in the plan, then it does the work on your machine.

Anthropic's advice is to start with tasks that have clear boundaries and to always review what Claude produces.

"Show me the plan before making changes."Claude, Getting started with Claude Cowork, 2:38

06Stay fresh, aim higher

Keep your setup fresh. Then aim higher.

Models get better every few months, so a setup that helped last time can start holding Claude back. Re-test it, and keep handing Claude bigger goals.

16

Re-test your skills at every model release.

Anthropic's official guidance, and Felix's own, is to be careful with skills. They often tell a model things that mattered for the last generation, and sometimes removing a skill gives better output.

This is the one that made me rethink my own setup. At every major model release I now run the task on the raw model and with the skill, and keep whichever does better.

"the half-life of a good skill is very short."Felix at 21:54
In the Reel
Silicon Valley Girl

Open on YouTube at 21:42

17

Give it the goal, not just the task.

Ask what you're hoping happens after the task, and give Claude that instead. Keep going up until you hit what the current model can't do. "Go make money" is too far, Felix admits, but reaching the ceiling tells you you're at the frontier, and you'll get more out of every new model.

If all you ask for is an email summary, you're not using what the new models can do.

"whatever outcome you're hoping for, give the model that task."Felix at 39:15
Silicon Valley Girl

Open on YouTube at 38:55

18

Go one level up.

On How I AI, Felix built a furniture planner for his house and caught himself typing in each piece by hand. So he went a step up and just told Claude what furniture he had. Then another: why tell it at all, when Claude could work out what he owns from his email?

He keeps asking that question, then spends his own time on the fun, creative part.

19

Tell Claude it's possible.

When Felix tries something at the edge of what a computer can do, he tells Claude that he knows it's possible, even when he doesn't know how. He says it gives Claude more confidence to go after the task, and it skips the back and forth about whether the idea is a good one.

20

Spend the time you save on your customers.

Building is cheap now. Felix thinks the secret to a good product hasn't changed: empathy for the user, for the use case, and for whether the person paying finds it valuable. AI just makes it much easier to act on.

"And I believe it's empathy."Felix at 32:10

07The long summary

The interview, in plain English.

What Felix said, in the order he said it, for a business owner who uses Claude every day. Each time links to that moment in the video.

  1. 0:00
    You're asking too little of AI.

    The cold open. If all you ask for is "summarize this email", Felix says you aren't benefiting from the new intelligence. Marina runs a 35-person team that uses Claude across the channel and wants to get further.

  2. 3:04
    What his workday looks like now.

    He calls himself a mix of product manager, engineering manager, people manager and data scientist. Most days he prototypes: the team talks an idea through, tells Claude to build all the variations, then critiques them. Building is cheap and attention isn't: "I can easily build a thousand. I can't show you a thousand." Good products have fewer buttons, and the signal he looks for is people getting it quickly.

  3. 5:44
    Automation versus augmentation.

    Automation saves time and money and is where most of AI's economic value is today. Augmentation, doing things you couldn't do before, is what interests him. In a company, how people changed their workflow impresses him more than what the tool does.

  4. 7:13
    How to judge creative work.

    Decide what good means first. His weather example: a lucky guess should fail and a well-sourced near miss should pass. Good writers and artists have a process, and Claude responds well to being asked to take the same steps a professional takes. That's why telling it to use Blender lifted its 3D work.

  5. 12:57
    How to catch confident mistakes.

    Adversarial reviews: five threads try to prove the work wrong, a judge decides which criticisms hold, and the critics can be a smaller model. Then two buckets. If you have no idea what good looks like, "Just throw more tokens at it." If you do, judge the result the way a client judges a contractor or a lawyer.

  6. 17:13
    The one job he trusts fully.

    Fact-checking. One agent, primary sources for every number, and he trusts the result 100%.

  7. 17:36
    Are we overcomplicating agents?

    Yes, he thinks. Models are generalists, so skip the cast of characters. Give Claude the full view of the business through well-documented tools and let it choose what to pull. Be careful with skills, because the half-life of a good skill is very short. Think about the model's experience: it wants raw data and a computer.

  8. 22:47
    "I'm just a conveyor belt for Claude."

    He used to hand Claude one crash at a time. Now Claude gets all the crash data and decides what to fix. It costs more to set up, and it pays off.

  9. 24:06
    How to get started.

    Download the desktop app, let Claude use your computer and its built-in browser, and add connectors only once a workflow has proved itself.

  10. 25:08
    Why "you're an expert" isn't enough.

    Role prompts were a 2024 habit and get you role play. Ask which tools a top professional uses and have Claude use them. On trust, the question for him is less what he won't trust Claude with and more what he won't trust one session with, and a session judging its own work is top of that list. Find an outside signal, or at least another session.

  11. 28:45
    How to stop being your company's bottleneck.

    Marina's feedback lives in Telegram and closing the loop is manual. Felix's answer is the three-round loop: assemble your feedback, condense it into principles, find the ones you don't know you hold, then feed the list into reviews. Run Claude side by side with your own reviews for a few rounds before you hand over. For a team he suggests Claude Tag, Claude inside Slack, where several people can talk to Claude at once.

  12. 31:51
    What's the moat now?

    It hasn't changed, he says: empathy for the user and the use case. Bad software, the kind you use because there's nothing better, should go away.

  13. 34:56
    Where the opportunities are.

    The software running hotels and airports is clunky, and cleaning it up is an untapped opportunity. Execution has become extremely cheap, so people who understand a business's needs, MBAs included, can now build software startups.

  14. 37:34
    The gap between what AI can do and how we use it.

    He agrees there's a capability overhang. Anthropic's CEO Dario Amodei talks about a data center of geniuses, and Felix sees his job as helping people make use of it. What we believe is possible holds us back more than the models do.

  15. 38:37
    Give Claude the goal beyond the task.

    Ask what you hope happens next and give the model that. Keep going until you find the limit of the current model, and you'll benefit from every new one.

  16. 39:51
    What to build and learn to stay valuable.

    For young engineers: build real things and a portfolio, show you can talk to customers, and learn to combine systems. When he hires, what you've made counts for more than your degree.

  17. 43:24
    What children should learn.

    How businesses work and how people collaborate. He admires the University of Waterloo's model, where students do several internships before they graduate.

  18. 46:54
    Life in five years.

    We keep underestimating how fast AI improves. He thinks today's AI products are like the phones before the smartphone. And a warning about this whole list: "all the advice I've given you today in about 10 weeks, we're probably going to have a new revision on all of it."

09What to do now

Pick one. Try it this week.

Reading these won't change much. Trying one on real work will. Here are three of Felix's methods as prompts you can paste into Cowork or any Claude chat.

The three-round feedback loop (tip 13)

Here is feedback I've given on my team's work: [paste it, or point to the folder or channel where it lives].

Round 1: Assemble all of it into one list, grouped by topic. Keep my exact words.
Round 2: Condense that list into the rules or principles I care about. Give one real example of my feedback for each.
Round 3: Now find the principles I might believe but don't know I believe: patterns in my feedback that I've never written down. Show the evidence for each.

Finish with one list I can paste into future reviews as my review principles.

The adversarial review (tips 3 and 4)

Here is my draft: [paste or attach it].

Fire off five separate threads. Give each one a single job: prove this draft is wrong. One checks every fact, one attacks the main argument, one looks for anything I've misunderstood, one writes the strongest counterargument, and one looks for what's missing.

Then act as the judge. List every criticism, say whether it holds up and why. Give me only the valid ones, most important first.

In a plain chat with no threads, open five chats with the same draft and one job each, then paste their notes into a sixth chat as the judge.

The fact-check (tip 6)

Check every fact in this [deck / post / document]. For every number I mention, find a primary source and check that it's a good primary source.

Give me a table: the claim, the source with its link, whether it holds up, and the fix if it doesn't.

Where to start

  • New to Cowork: tips 12 and 15. Download the desktop app, point it at one messy folder and ask for the plan first.
  • Your prompts start with "you are an expert": tips 1 and 2.
  • You don't trust what Claude hands back: tips 3 to 7. Run the fact-check on your next deck.
  • You're the bottleneck on your team: tips 13 and 14. Gather a month of your feedback and run the three rounds.
  • You've built a lot of skills or long instructions: tip 16. Test them against the raw model this week.

See what's possible. Then build it.
