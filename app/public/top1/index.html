01 · Build

Harness engineering.

The model is maybe thirty percent of your system. The harness is everything wrapped around it, and that is where your quality actually comes from.

Two teams using the identical model get wildly different results, and people assume it is the prompt. It is not. It is the harness: what context gets assembled and in what order, which model handles which step, what the guardrails reject, how many loops the agent is allowed before it gives up, how many tool calls it can burn, and what condition tells it it is finished. Swap the model and a good harness stays good. Swap the harness and everything changes.

This is the first concept because it reframes the whole job. You are not prompt engineering. You are building the machine the prompt runs inside.

02 · Build

Evaluations.

Vibes do not scale. An eval is the difference between believing your system got better and knowing it did.

You need a golden set: real inputs with known-good outputs that you grade every change against. You need regression tests so today's improvement does not quietly break last month's fix. You need adversarial cases, the weird and hostile inputs that expose what your happy path hides. And when you use a model to grade a model, LLM-as-judge, you have to align that judge against human grading first, or you are just measuring one model's taste.

Most people skip this and ship on feel. It works right up until it does not, and by then nobody can tell you which change caused the drop.

03 · Build

Retrieval architecture.

Most bad AI answers are not a model problem. They are a retrieval problem wearing a model's clothes.

If the right passage never made it into the context window, no model on earth answers correctly. So the architecture matters: how you chunk documents, which embedding model you use, whether you combine semantic search with keyword search because pure vectors miss exact terms like error codes and product names, whether you rerank the top candidates before spending context on them, and how fresh the index is. Stale retrieval is confidently wrong retrieval.

Chunking, hybrid search and reranking are the three levers that move quality most, and almost nobody tunes all three.

04 · Build

Retrieval evals.

Evaluating the answer is not the same as evaluating what the answer was built from. These are two separate scoreboards.

Retrieval has its own metrics. Recall asks whether the right documents were fetched at all. Precision asks how much junk came with them. Grounding asks whether the generated answer is actually supported by what was retrieved, rather than the model filling gaps from memory. Attribution and citation quality ask whether the sources it points at genuinely say what it claims they say.

Splitting these out is what lets you fix the real problem. A grounded answer built on the wrong documents and a hallucinated answer built on the right ones look identical from the outside, and they need opposite fixes.

05 · Run

Observability.

When an agent gives a bad answer in production, can you replay exactly what happened? If not, you are not running a system, you are hoping.

Traces and spans give you the full path of a request: every model call, every tool call, every retrieval, nested and timed. On top of that you watch tokens, latency, error rates, and drift, the slow shift in your inputs or your model's behaviour that nobody notices until quality has already gone.

The tell is simple. Ask someone how they debug a bad output. If the answer involves re-running it manually and seeing what happens, they have no observability.

06 · Run

Safety engineering.

Any text your system reads is text that can instruct it. That single sentence is the whole discipline.

Prompt injection is not a jailbreak game, it is an architectural problem: a web page, a PDF, a support ticket or an email can carry instructions your agent will happily follow. Defending it means treating retrieved content as untrusted data rather than commands, preventing data leakage across users and tenants, and drawing hard permission boundaries so the agent simply cannot reach the tool that does the damage.

The rule that survives everything: never rely on the model refusing. Rely on the model not having access.

07 · Run

Cost attribution.

Knowing your monthly model bill is not cost control. It is a number you can do nothing with.

Attribution means slicing spend the way the business is actually shaped: per feature, per workflow, per tenant, per user journey. That is what lets you say this one feature costs eleven cents a run and earns nothing, or this customer's usage is quietly unprofitable, or this retry loop tripled our spend on Tuesday.

This is the concept that gets you taken seriously outside engineering, because it converts tokens into the only unit a business cares about: margin per thing we do.

08 · Run

Production failure modes.

Experienced operators do not debug faster because they are smarter. They debug faster because they have seen the same five failures before.

Agents fail in patterns: hallucinated tool calls to functions that do not exist, malformed JSON that breaks the parser downstream, stale retrieval serving last quarter's answer, runaway agents looping until the budget is gone, and the worst one, silent eval regressions where quality drops and every dashboard stays green because nothing technically errored.

Learn the catalogue and you stop treating each incident as novel. You start recognising it in the first minute instead of the first hour.

09 · Frontier

The iron triangle of trade-offs.

Latency, quality, cost. You get to optimise two. Every serious decision in an AI system is you choosing which one to give up.

Want a better answer? Bigger model, more reasoning, more retrieval, so it costs more and takes longer. Want it instant? Smaller model or aggressive caching, so quality moves. Want it cheap? Something gives, always. And this plays out across the whole inference stack, not just the model choice: how you chunk, how many candidates you rerank, how many agent loops you allow, whether you batch.

The people inside the labs think in this triangle constantly. Once you see it, every architecture argument becomes legible, because you can name which corner each side is defending.

10 · Frontier

Model adaptation strategies.

Four ways to make a general model do your specific job, and knowing which one to reach for is the whole skill.

In-context learning, just showing it examples in the prompt, is free and instant and where you should always start. Retrieval gives it knowledge it never had, and it is what you want when the facts change. Fine-tuning teaches it a behaviour or a format, not facts, and people reach for it far too early. Distillation trains a small cheap model to imitate a big expensive one, which is how you buy back latency and cost once quality is already proven.

The mistake almost everyone makes is fine-tuning to fix a knowledge gap that retrieval would have closed in an afternoon. Get this decision right and you save months.

11 · What to do now

Pick one. Find it in your own work.

Reading ten articles changes nothing on its own. Reading one and then going to look for that concept inside something you have built changes how you see every AI system after it.

  • If you have shipped nothing yet: start at 01 and 02. Harness and evals are the two that make everything after them possible.
  • If you have a RAG app that is "mostly good": go straight to 03 and 04. It is almost always retrieval, and you probably have no retrieval metrics.
  • If something is live and you are nervous about it: 05, 06 and 08. Traces, permission boundaries, and the failure catalogue.
  • If you are in the room where the money is discussed: 07 and 09. Cost attribution and the iron triangle are the two that make you fluent with the business.

Then use the words. Half of being in the top 1% is being able to say "that is a retrieval eval problem, not a model problem" in a meeting, and being right.
