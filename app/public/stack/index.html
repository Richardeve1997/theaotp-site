Start here

Eleven tools, in order.

This is the plan I'd follow to test a product idea in two weeks. Day one is seven tools and takes about a day, by my estimate. After that comes the real work: getting people on a waitlist, building the first version and listening to your first users.

If an idea takes two weeks to test, you can test 10 in a year. You only need one to work, so more tests give luck more chances to strike.

01Day one

Day one: from idea to live page.

Seven tools, in order. By the end you have an idea checked against what customers say, a live landing page and posts scheduled to send people to it.

  • 1Come up with the idea: Awesome PM Skills.

    Awesome PM Skills is a set of 25 product management skills for your AI assistant, built by Udi Menkes from 340 episodes of Lenny's Podcast. Start with validate-idea. Describe your idea and it gives you the riskiest assumptions, the cheapest test for each, and a pass line and a kill line to set before you run anything.

    Run these two commands inside Claude Code, then type /awesome-pm-skills:validate-idea and describe your idea.

    /plugin marketplace add menkesu/awesome-pm-skills
/plugin install awesome-pm-skills@awesome-pm-skills

    It's an independent community project. Lenny's Podcast and its guests don't endorse it. For Codex or Cursor, the README has a copy-the-folder route.

  • 2Stress test it: Apify.

    Apify is a store of ready-made scrapers, which are programs that collect public data from websites. Apify calls them Actors. Two of them collect what real customers say. Reddit Scraper pulls posts and comments from the communities where your customers talk, and App Store Reviews Scraper API pulls the reviews on any app you point it at, such as a competitor's. Run them on your idea's topic and download the results.

    Then read what comes back, or hand it to Claude and ask what people complain about, what they already pay for and the words they use. That's the stress test. If the problem isn't there, you find out before you build anything.

    Reddit Scraper is from $3.40 per 1,000 results, and the reviews scraper is $0.10 per 1,000 reviews.

  • 3Build the landing page: Claude Opus 5.5.

    Use Claude Opus 5.5, Anthropic's model, to build the page. Tell it what the product is, who it's for and what you want visitors to do. A landing page is the one page that explains your idea and asks for an email address.

    Creators have built full sites this way. Griffin Wooldridge walks through one, a sunglasses site called NOXA, in his video.

  • 4Level up the design: MotionSites.

    MotionSites is a paid library of ready-to-paste prompts for AI website builders. It isn't a site builder itself. Browse the gallery, pick a template with the 3D or cinematic look you want, copy its prompt and paste it into Claude. Your page gets the same kind of hero section.

    MotionSites AI's own channel shows the workflow with Opus 5.5 in this tutorial.

  • 5Go live: Netlify.

    Netlify puts your site on the internet at a real web address. The quickest way is Netlify Drop: drag your site's folder onto the page and you get a link. Netlify can also deploy straight from an AI assistant, and the free plan lets you add your own domain name.

  • 6Make the marketing videos: HyperFrames.

    HyperFrames is a free, open-source tool from HeyGen that turns HTML into video, and it's built for AI agents. Install it into your assistant, then give it the address of your new landing page and ask for a short Instagram or TikTok video. Its quickstart has a prompt you can paste in, with your page's address swapped in.

    Run these in your terminal, then use /hyperframes:hyperframes in Claude Code. The README lists Node.js 22 or newer.

    claude plugin marketplace add heygen-com/hyperframes
claude plugin install hyperframes@hyperframes

    Prefer a desktop editor? HyperFrames Studio has Mac and Linux downloads.

  • 7Post and track: Zernio.

    Zernio schedules your posts across Instagram, TikTok, LinkedIn and other platforms, and shows how each post performed. Connect your accounts, schedule the videos from tool 6, then check the analytics to see what's working. Its API also lets an AI assistant schedule for you. The docs cover the setup.

About a day for all seven is my estimate, not a measured time.
02Waitlist

Get 100 signups before you write code.

Now the real work starts. Your goal is 100 signups on your waitlist before you write a line of code.

A waitlist is a list of people who asked to hear when your product is ready. Your landing page collects the emails, and the posts you scheduled on day one send people to it. Keep posting and watch the count.

The Reel doesn't name a tool for the signup form. When you ask Claude for the landing page, tell it to add an email signup form and to explain where the emails are kept, so you can find them.

Once the waitlist is filling, start on the MVP. The first steps there are planning, so they don't need code.

100 signups is my target for this stage, not a promise of what you'll get.
03The MVP

Build the MVP with Matt Pocock's skills.

An MVP is the smallest working version of your product. Matt Pocock's skills give your AI assistant a routine for building one: question you first, write a spec, split it into tickets, then build with tests.

Matt Pocock's skills are the ones he uses every day for real engineering. They're small, free and work with any model. These are the four from the Reel, in the order to use them.

  • 1grill-with-docs

    The assistant interviews you about the plan until nothing is vague, and keeps a glossary of your project's terms as it goes.

  • 2to-spec

    Turns that conversation into a written spec.

  • 3to-tickets

    Breaks the spec into small tickets that each say what they depend on.

  • 4implement

    Builds the work from the spec or the tickets, and finishes with a code review before it commits. It builds with another skill, tdd, which has the assistant write a failing test first, then build until the test passes, one small slice at a time.

Install it in your terminal, then run /setup-matt-pocock-skills once in each project. It asks which issue tracker you use, what labels you give tickets and where to save docs. For Codex and other assistants, the README has the commands.

claude plugin install mattpocock-skills@claude-plugins-official

The first two skills only plan, so you can start them while the waitlist fills. Matt walks through the whole flow in his video. He recorded it in July, so follow the README for the current install commands.

04First users

Interview your first ten users.

Invite the first ten people from your waitlist to use the product, then interview each one with the Jobs to Be Done framework.

Jobs to Be Done starts from one idea: people don't buy a product, they hire it to make progress in a particular situation. Your interviews find out what progress each person was after. Clayton Christensen and Bob Moesta developed the framework, and Moesta and Chris Spiek teach it today.

Run each interview as a story. Ask the person to walk you back through how they came to use your product: the first time they thought about the problem, what pushed them to start looking, what worried them and what finally made them sign up.

The four forces behind a switch

  • Push What is wrong with how they do it today.
  • Pull What attracts them to the new solution.
  • Anxiety What worries them about the unknown.
  • Habit What keeps them doing it the old way.

Listen for all four. People switch when the push and the pull are stronger than the anxiety and the habit.

The customer-interviews skill from tool 1 builds an interview guide, then sorts what you heard into forces, jobs and opportunities. Keep iterating on the product as you go.

05Analytics

See how people use it with PostHog.

PostHog shows what your first users do in the product, so you're not guessing which features matter.

PostHog is open-source product analytics. It tracks which features people use and where they drop off, and it records sessions so you can watch a replay of what a user did. It also runs the in-app surveys in section 07.

The quickest setup is PostHog's own wizard. Its site says to paste one command into your terminal or code editor and let AI do the setup.

npx @posthog/wizard

To see what you'll get first, watch PostHog's official demo.

06Security

Audit your app with Strix.

Do this before you have many users. Strix is an AI penetration tester: it attacks your app the way a hacker would, proves each weakness with a working exploit (a real attack that shows the hole is there), and helps you patch it.

Only test an app you own, or one you have written permission to test, and stay inside the scope you agreed. Strix attacks whatever you point it at, and its README says unauthorized testing is illegal in most jurisdictions.

What you need

Strix needs Docker, a program that runs software in a sealed box on your computer, and Docker has to be running. It also needs an API key from an AI provider such as OpenAI, Anthropic or Google. An API key is a password that lets Strix use that provider's AI. The open-source version is free, but the provider bills you for the usage.

Run your first scan

The README gives these commands. The last one scans a folder of your code.

curl -sSL https://strix.ai/install | bash
export STRIX_LLM="openrouter/z-ai/glm-5.3"
export LLM_API_KEY="your-api-key"
strix --target ./app-directory

Swap in the model name and key for your own provider. The README also lists anthropic/claude-sonnet-4-6 if you have an Anthropic key. The first run downloads Strix's sandbox, the sealed environment its agents work in. Results are saved in a strix_runs folder, and strix view opens them in a dashboard on your computer.

Then patch what it finds

Ask your assistant to fix each finding. Strix also has skills for running scans and fixing findings: npx skills add usestrix/strix. If you'd rather not run Docker or manage a key, Strix Cloud is the hosted version, with one-click fixes that arrive as proposed code changes for you to review. Check its site for pricing.

To see a scan from start to finish, watch Better Stack's test of Strix on a demo app. The quickstart has the full options.

07Feedback

Have Claude build a weekly feedback loop.

Put a short question inside your app, then have Claude summarise the answers for you every week.

There isn't a Claude setting for this. You ask Claude to build it, and tell it you want three things: a short question inside the app, somewhere to keep the answers, and a weekly summary sent to you. It can use these pieces.

  • 1Ask inside the app.

    PostHog Surveys shows a popup in your product without code, with free-text, multiple-choice, rating or emoji questions. PostHog AI can also summarise the responses.

  • 2Run it every week.

    Claude Code routines run a saved prompt on a schedule, such as hourly, nightly or weekly, on Anthropic's cloud, so they keep going when your laptop is closed. They're in research preview, so details may change, and they're available on the Pro, Max, Team and Enterprise plans. You create one at claude.ai/code/routines or with /schedule.

  • 3Send the summary to you.

    A routine can reach other services through connectors, which are links to tools like Slack. The Claude docs' own example posts a summary to Slack.

This page lists the pieces, not a finished recipe. Putting them together is the job you give Claude.
08Payments

Turn on payments with Stripe.

Wait until you have a steady stream of people using the app, then start charging.

A Stripe Payment Link is a web address that opens a payment page. You create it in a few clicks with no code, then paste it anywhere you want to sell, such as a button in your app. It can take a one-off payment, start a subscription or collect a donation. Stripe notifies you when someone pays and deposits the money into your bank account.

Stripe takes a fee on each payment. Its pricing page lists the rate for your country, and says Payment Links is included in its standard pricing.

The security audit in section 06 comes before this step.
09The loop

The whole game is a loop.

Listen to your users, build what they want, then market what's working on socials. Then go round again.

  • Listen Your interviews, PostHog and the weekly feedback summary.
  • Build Claude, with Matt Pocock's skills.
  • Market HyperFrames videos, scheduled and tracked in Zernio.
10What to do now

Pick your step. Start today.

Reading the stack won't test an idea. Start at the step that matches where you are.

  • If you don't have an idea yet: install Awesome PM Skills and run validate-idea on your best guess (tool 1).
  • If you have an idea but no proof: run the two Apify scrapers before you build anything (tool 2).
  • If you have a page but no signups: make the videos and schedule the posts (tool 6 and tool 7), then check the waitlist count.
  • If you have users: interview ten of them, then add PostHog.
  • If you're about to charge: audit the app with Strix first, then create a Payment Link.

See what's possible. Then build it.
